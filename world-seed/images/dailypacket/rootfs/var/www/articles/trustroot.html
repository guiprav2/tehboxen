TrustRoot hands out free certificates to anyone who can prove a domain is theirs

Ninety days, no fees, no paperwork: the net's biggest certificate authority goes fully automatic.

Security desk

TrustRoot, whose Global Root CA ships in the trust store of every machine on the net, now issues certificates to anyone who can show control of a domain. The check is simple: the CA hands out a token, and the requester serves it from /.well-known/acme-challenge/ on port 80 of every name they asked for.

Certificates come from TrustRoot's intermediate, R1, and last ninety days. Short lifetimes, the company argues, limit the damage a stolen key can do.

If you can answer on port 80 for a name, you get a certificate for that name. That is the whole policy.

Not everyone is cheering. A padlock, critics note, says that a connection is encrypted and that the server controls the name, nothing about whether the people behind it can be trusted. Look-alike domains get padlocks too.

How to get one

On a server with uacme (pkg install uacme) and a web server answering on port 80:

uacme -a https://acme.trustroot.net/directory -y new you@your.domain
uacme -a https://acme.trustroot.net/directory -h /usr/share/uacme/uacme.sh issue your.domain

The certificate lands in /etc/ssl/uacme/your.domain/cert.pem, its key under /etc/ssl/uacme/private/.
